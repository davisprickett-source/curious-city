# Portland's Hidden Stairways: Comprehensive Research Document

## Executive Summary

Portland, Oregon possesses one of the most extensive networks of public stairways in the United States, with approximately 200 documented staircases woven throughout the city's hilly terrain. These stairways, constructed primarily at the turn of the 20th century, represent a vanished era of pedestrian-focused urban planning and worker mobility. Today, they serve as hidden architectural treasures that connect neighborhoods, provide efficient shortcuts through challenging topography, and offer a tangible link to Portland's industrial and streetcar-era past.

---

## 1. Historical Background: When and Why Portland's Stairways Were Built

### The Turn-of-the-Century Construction Boom

Portland's public stairway network emerged during a specific historical moment when the city's urban development intersected with unique transportation and geographical challenges. The area is notable for some 400 public stairs originally constructed at the turn of the 20th century, with the most concentrated development occurring between 1910 and 1920.

The Alameda neighborhood provides a well-documented example of this construction era. In 1912, the Warren Construction Company built concrete stairways anchored into the slope of Gravelly Hill (now known as Alameda Ridge). These concrete flights were designed to connect the brand-new Olmsted Park subdivision above with early Fremont Street and what developers hoped would eventually become new neighborhoods below.

### The Streetcar Era and Pedestrian Culture

When these stairways were built in 1912, most Portlanders relied on walking and streetcars to get around, with horses used to move freight. A dispersed system of public stairways made practical sense, particularly navigating landscapes like Gravelly Hill. The majority of Portland's oldest neighborhoods owe their location and growth to the development of street railways, with the streetcar defining the way Portland looks today, particularly during the peak Portland streetcar era of 1915-1920.

Portland's Irvington Neighborhood is renowned as an exemplar of a classic early twentieth century "streetcar suburb." The city developed as an interlinked network of neighborhood communities, each with quirks, insularities, and personalities, along with thriving little commercial districts reincarnated from the streetcar era.

### Workers and Mills: The Linnton Example

The industrial character of early Portland necessitated efficient pedestrian connections between workers' homes and their workplaces. The Linnton neighborhood provides the most dramatic example of this worker-oriented infrastructure.

Linnton's first lumber mill was built in 1904 and sold to Clark & Wilson. By 1914, the population grew from 400 to 2,000 as shipbuilding companies and lumber mills were established. At its incorporation in 1910, Linnton was a company town for Clark-Wilson and West Oregon lumber mills, and the Columbia Engineering Works shipyard. Immigrants from Japan, the Philippines, Italy, East India, and Czechoslovakia came to work in the mills and lived in company housing along the railroad tracks.

The stairways in Linnton's hillside connected workers' homes directly to the lumber mills below. In neighborhoods like Linnton, if you lived in adjoining houses, you'd just pop out of your house, hit the staircase, and be at work in five minutes at a lumber mill. This created what observers describe as "a great pedestrian culture."

One of Linnton's most distinctive features is 107th Avenue, possibly the only street in Portland that is actually a stairway. When Linnton's mills closed and downtown vanished, so did the kids who used to climb these stairs to the old Linnton School.

### The Depression Era: WPA Contributions

A second significant wave of stairway construction occurred during the Great Depression through the Works Progress Administration (WPA). A graceful double staircase of hand-hewn stone was built by Works Progress Administration crews during the Depression era at what is now Joseph Wood Hill Park on Rocky Butte.

Rocky Butte Scenic Historic District, including approach roads and a viewing area on the hill, was constructed between 1934 and 1939 by the State Emergency Relief Administration (SERA) and federal Works Progress Administration. When completion was announced in September 1939, work on the project had provided employment for as many as 700 men at times. WPA workers built an impressive viewing area on the summit with a massive stone parapet wall encircling it, offering panoramic vistas of the Columbia River valley and Portland.

Portland designated the site as Joseph Wood Hill Park in 1988, and the 72 hand-hewn stone steps connect a closed park road to a panoramic hilltop. The park is named after Joseph Wood Hill, who ran the Hill Military Academy that was located on Rocky Butte from 1931 to 1959.

Other WPA-era construction includes the Washington Park steps, probably from the 1930s, and steps into the International Rose Test Garden built in 1941 as part of a WPA project. One notable Washington Park staircase is a hand-built set of 203 stairs that scales a forested cliff, last rebuilt in 1984. In 1940, someone named R.F. Hurlburt, general foreman, sent in a bid to rebuild the stairs for $768.80.

## 2. Specific Examples: Named Locations and Notable Stairways

### Northeast Portland: Alameda Ridge

Alameda Ridge represents the most concentrated collection of public stairways in Portland, with eleven staircases ranging from 30 to 127 steps. This "hike" boasts a combined 400-800 stair-steps crisscrossing a ridge scattered across 30 blocks in Northeast Portland. Alameda Ridge cuts through the neighborhoods of Rose City, Alameda, and Beaumont-Wilshire.

The eleven staircases were built in 1912 by Warren Construction Company as concrete flights anchored into the slope of the ridge. One set of steps lands just west of NE 32nd Avenue, coming down from Alameda Terrace. Several stairs, such as the ones near NE 32nd Avenue and Fremont Street, are literally hidden in the hedges.

The 30th Avenue stairs were noted in the May 1981 citywide Historic Resource Inventory and are labeled as "Olmsted Park Public Right of Way." A gravel pit stretched downslope beyond today's Klickitat Street and spanned from NE 33rd to NE 31st.

As Portland's gridded layout rapidly expanded eastward at the beginning of the 20th century, Alameda Ridge posed a challenge, as simply wrapping the grid around the six-mile long rise posed numerous difficulties, particularly when most people got around primarily on foot. The City of Portland constructed these staircases to connect the half dozen neighborhoods being developed along the ridge, and remains the authority maintaining the steps to this day.

Stunning homes (many historic), breathtaking views, and eleven staircases give Portland's otherworldly NE Alameda Ridge neighborhood its special vibe.

### Northwest Portland: The Alphabet District and Westover Terraces

Northwest Portland features a dense network of stairways connecting the flatter streets of the Alphabet District with the higher elevations around Westover Terraces in the West Hills. The area is referred to as the Northwest District, Nob Hill, or the Alphabet District, because the named streets follow the alphabet, beginning with Burnside and ending with Thurman.

In the Alphabet District, except for Hoyt Street, all streets between G and Q in Nob Hill end in a staircase. For example, there's a stairway at Glisan at 24th to Westover with 51 steps, and another at Summit Avenue south of Summit Court to Westover with 157 steps.

The Thurman Stairway is named after the cross street at the lower end of the stairway. Many stairways can be found on dead-end streets near Wallace Park in Northwest Portland. Parks within the Northwest District include Couch Park (1977) and Wallace Park (1920). Many of these city staircases are in neighborhoods adjacent to Forest Park, providing pedestrian access through Portland's hilly Northwest terrain.

### Southeast Portland: Mount Tabor

Mount Tabor features multiple significant stairways throughout the park:

- There are 293 stairs total from the SE Yamhill/SE 69th Street entrance to the summit, though these stairs are broken by paved roads and a short dirt trail section
- The Blue Trail includes a 96-step stairway between Reservoirs 5 and 6
- Two wide sets of stairs with a combined 413 steps are located at Southeast 69th Avenue and Park Drive
- The main stairs address is listed as 1062 SE 69th Ave
- From Mount Tabor's epic 282 steps to the glass cupola atop 115 steps in Pioneer Courthouse, these stairways feature details with amazing stories and something unique to offer urban explorers

### North Portland: Cathedral Park

Cathedral Park features a stairway with 41 steps under the St. Johns Bridge. The stairs descend from Edison Street down 41 wide, graceful steps to a broad circle with a low wall, providing a scenic spot to admire the arched underside of the St. Johns Bridge. The Cathedral Park staircase is noted as potentially being "the most photographed staircase in Portland."

### Downtown: Pioneer Courthouse

The Pioneer Courthouse, a National Historic Landmark and the oldest extant federal building in the Pacific Northwest, was completed in 1875. The building has 115 total stairs: 9 steps outside to the front door; 30 to the second floor; 26 to the third floor; 18 to the fourth floor; and 32 to the cupola.

The historic octagonal cupola was originally constructed in 1875 and has remained unchanged. The octagonal structure has a two-light, round-arched double-hung window on each face, providing glimpses of downtown Portland and beyond in each direction. The building is open for self-guided tours between 9:00 AM and 4:00 PM, Monday through Friday (except holidays). The cupola provides a 360-degree view of the downtown area, with views of Pioneer Square across the street.

### Rocky Butte

Rocky Butte features notable stairways, including the entrance to the viewing area constructed by the WPA during the 1930s. The graceful double staircase of hand-hewn stone consists of 72 steps connecting a closed park road to a panoramic hilltop at Joseph Wood Hill Park.

## 3. Numbers: The Scale of Portland's Stairway Network

### Total Count

The exact number of public stairways in Portland varies slightly depending on the source and counting methodology:

- Oregon Public Broadcasting estimates the city has 200 stairways
- Author Laura O. Foster documented 196 public stairways in her book "The Portland Stairs Book"
- Some sources cite 180+ public stairways
- At least 200 publicly accessible staircases exist in the city of Portland, Oregon

The most commonly cited estimate is approximately 200 public stairways in Portland.

### Original Construction vs. Remaining Stairways

The area is notable for some 400 public stairs originally constructed at the turn of the 20th century. However, this figure appears to refer specifically to the Alameda Ridge area, which has seen significant preservation of its original staircases.

Portland has two dozen outdoor public stairways that consist of at least 100 steps. Laura Foster's "Portland Stair Walks" features 18 urban adventures with 142 staircases, along with hidden paths and pedestrian bridges throughout the city's neighborhoods.

The Alameda Ridge area alone features eleven staircases ranging from 30 to 127 steps, with a combined 400-800 stair-steps across the ridge. The total count for individual staircases in this area is 844 stairs according to one documentation effort.

### Geographic Distribution

Portland's stairways are distributed across the city's hilly terrain:

- Northeast Portland: Alameda Ridge area (11 staircases, 844 total steps)
- Northwest Portland: Alphabet District and Westover Terraces (numerous staircases between streets G and Q)
- Southeast Portland: Mount Tabor (multiple stairways totaling over 400 steps)
- North Portland: Cathedral Park (41 steps)
- Southwest Portland: Washington Park area (including 203-step forested cliff stairway)
- Rocky Butte: Joseph Wood Hill Park (72 hand-hewn stone steps)

## 4. Urban Development Context: Why Portland Needed Stairways

### Topographical Challenges

Portland's public stairways are attractions that weave history, architectural design together with public utility in this hilly city. Several public staircases have been constructed for pedestrians to connect various parts of neighborhoods, stemming from two main factors: the geology of the area and the timeframe in which the neighborhood's layout was originally planned and developed.

As Portland's gridded layout rapidly expanded eastward at the beginning of the 20th century, topographic features like Alameda Ridge posed significant challenges. Simply wrapping the grid around the six-mile long rise posed numerous difficulties, particularly when most people got around primarily on foot.

The grid expanded across rivers and hills with minimal deviation in East Portland. Unlike other cities where grids warped with topography, Portland flattened and extended its grid across flatlands with consistency. However, in areas with challenging terrain, streets follow natural topography instead of cardinal directions, with some neighborhoods built on steep terrain featuring fragmented streets, steep inclines, switchbacks, and cul-de-sacs where the grid was impractical.

Topography and lack of stormwater infrastructure are two technical factors that can make building sidewalks much more challenging and expensive in Portland, requiring more extensive engineering and construction during the development review process.

### Pre-Automobile Urban Planning

Before city planners gave Portland the tram to get around, they gave the city stairs. When built in 1912, most Portlanders relied on walking and streetcars to get around, and horses were used to move freight. Each of Portland's stairways were built at different times for different reasons. Most of the stairs are extremely steep; they were installed to help walkers get from one elevation to another via the most direct route.

A dispersed system of public stairways just made sense, particularly navigating up and down landscapes like Gravelly Hill (today's Alameda Ridge). Public stairs offer more direct routes between neighborhood streets in a city where pedestrian infrastructure was paramount.

The City of Portland constructed staircases to connect the half dozen neighborhoods being developed along ridges and hillsides. Some stairs used to serve as main thoroughfares before streets were laid, creating pedestrian pathways that predated automobile-oriented infrastructure.

### The Impact of Urban Renewal (1950s-1970s)

In the 1950s, urban renewal came to Portland, and the once walkable neighborhoods were severed by high-speed roads and elevated parkways. This period significantly impacted Portland's pedestrian infrastructure, though many historic stairways survived.

The Albina neighborhood provides the most dramatic example of urban renewal's impact. Beginning in 1956, Albina saw a series of three massive construction projects that destroyed homes and displaced hundreds in N/NE Portland:

1. **Veterans Memorial Coliseum (1950s)**: Construction resulted in the destruction of 450 homes in the predominantly Black neighborhood.

2. **Interstate 5 Construction (1960s)**: I-5 was routed through Central Albina, cutting through the center of the Albina community. Albina's Eliot neighborhood alone lost half of its residents—3,000 people—to involuntary displacement between 1960 and 1970.

3. **Emanuel Hospital Expansion**: Emanuel Hospital cleared land of more than 200 properties, and the promised 180-300 replacement housing units were never built.

In total, at least 349 parcels were secured, 1,573 residents (including 336 families) and 289 businesses were relocated, and 445 buildings were demolished in various urban renewal projects. All told, over 1,100 homes in Albina were demolished by 1973, and the overwhelming majority of the losses were felt by Black homeowners.

In Linnton, downtown was bulldozed in the 1960s to make room for a four-lane highway (Highway 30), destroying the pedestrian infrastructure that had connected hillside homes to the mills below.

While some stairways undoubtedly disappeared during this urban renewal period, the exact number lost to highway construction and urban redevelopment has not been comprehensively documented. The surviving stairways represent both a remnant of Portland's pre-automobile pedestrian culture and a testament to the resilience of neighborhood-scale infrastructure.

## 5. Current Status: Use, Preservation, and Deterioration

### Active Use by Pedestrians and Fitness Enthusiasts

Portland's public stairways remain actively used today, serving multiple contemporary purposes beyond their original function as utilitarian neighborhood connectors.

**Pedestrian Access**: Public stairs offer more direct routes between neighborhood streets. The public staircases of Portland are described as fun and funky hidden passageways nestled between houses, snaking through ivy and often rising high above the street. Portland has 180+ public stairways that connect neighborhoods across its varied topography.

**Fitness and Exercise**: The Alameda staircase is described as great for cross-training, with riser height and distance of 30 steps that are just right for wind sprints. The stairways are often promoted as offering "the best workout you can get without putting on gym clothes." Portland has some 400 public stairs originally constructed at the turn of the 20th century, which have become a favorite attraction for walkers and runners.

**Photography and Social Media**: The Cathedral Park staircase is noted as potentially being "the most photographed staircase in Portland," with its 41 wide, graceful steps leading to a spot under the St. Johns Bridge providing a scenic backdrop.

### Maintenance Responsibilities and Challenges

**City Responsibility**: The City of Portland constructed these staircases to connect neighborhoods and remains the authority maintaining the steps to this day. Every stair, porch, and attachment to stairs or porches must be so constructed as to be safe to use and capable of supporting the loads to which it is subjected and must be kept in sound condition and good repair, including replacement as necessary of flooring, treads, risers, and stringers that evidence excessive wear and are broken, warped, or loose.

**Property Owner Responsibilities**: For infrastructure in rights-of-way, the owners of land abutting any street in Portland are responsible for constructing, reconstructing, maintaining and repairing the sidewalks, curbs, driveways and parking strips abutting or immediately adjacent to said land, and property owners are liable for damages resulting from defective conditions or failure to keep such areas in safe condition.

After receiving a complaint of hazardous sidewalk conditions, PBOT inspectors investigate the specific complaint as well as the conditions of all nearby sidewalks. PBOT then issues a legal affidavit called a Sidewalk Repair Notice to all adjacent and responsible property owners. Portland City Code allows 60 days for repairing the sidewalk after receiving a notice.

Hazardous conditions that trigger repair notices include:
- Sidewalks with a vertical step separation equal to one-half inch or more
- Sidewalks with openings, cracks, or gaps in the sidewalk equal to one-half an inch or wider
- Sidewalks where the surface is chipped or deteriorated to a depth of one-half of an inch or more

Adjacent property owners may repair the sidewalk themselves, hire a contractor, or allow the city to make repairs and bill them. In the event timely repairs are not made, PBOT will hire a private contractor to make the repairs, then bill the property owner for the cost.

**Volunteer Efforts**: The Terwilliger stairs are maintained by volunteers, demonstrating community engagement in preserving these historical features.

### Restoration and Deterioration

**Restoration Examples**:
- The Washington Park staircase with 203 stairs that scales a forested cliff was last rebuilt in 1984. In 1940, R.F. Hurlburt, general foreman, sent in a bid to rebuild the stairs for $768.80.
- The Alameda stairs have been documented through newly re-discovered stairway images from 1913, showing the 112-year-old public stairways built in 1912 by Warren Construction Company.
- Portland made the site at Rocky Butte into Joseph Wood Hill Park in 1988, preserving the hand-hewn stone WPA staircase.

**Hidden and Deteriorating Stairways**:
- Many stairways are narrow, steep and moss-covered, requiring careful searching to find them
- An old stairway fell into disrepair when a crack opened up and a seedling sprouted in the middle of the steps—that seedling is now a full-grown tree
- Some old stairways fell into disrepair when newer entrances were built
- Several stairs near NE 32nd Avenue and Fremont Street are literally hidden in the hedges

**Preservation Guidelines**: Adhering to preservation guidelines set forth by heritage conservation organizations ensures that Portland's historic staircases retain their intrinsic value for years to come. The city's stairways have gained attention through documentation efforts, including Laura O. Foster's guidebooks about Portland's staircases.

## 6. Cultural Significance: Landmarks, Tourism, and Community Identity

### Documentation and Guidebooks

Laura O. Foster has emerged as Portland's premier chronicler of the city's public stairways. She has hiked nearly every one of Portland's 180+ public stairways, uncovering forgotten tidbits of history along the way.

**"Portland Stair Walks"**: Foster's eighth guidebook about the Pacific Northwest, this book features 18 urban adventures on foot (or even bike) with:
- 142 staircases
- 22 hidden paths
- 26 bridges
- 2 tunnels
- 1 island
- 1 floating sidewalk
- 1 aerial tram
- Several beaches

**"The Portland Stairs Book"**: In this pocket-sized guide, Foster has gathered the best and most interesting of Portland's 196 public staircases. From Mount Tabor's epic 282 steps to the glass cupola atop 115 steps in Pioneer Courthouse, the book features details on twenty outdoor stairs that have amazing stories and something unique to offer an urban explorer.

Foster crafts routes designed to get urban walkers to places others often miss exploring, and her walks weave geology, architecture, history, ethnobotany, and horticulture into a story about each place. A former urban-walks guide for the Multnomah Athletic Club, she leads occasional walking tours for nonprofits and local government agencies.

### Urban Exploration and Identity

Portland's public stairways have become emblematic of the city's character as a pedestrian-friendly, quirky, historically conscious community. The stairways are described as:

The popularity of Foster's guidebooks and walking tours demonstrates that people actively seek out these stairways, transforming utilitarian infrastructure into destination landmarks. ## 7. Key Takeaways for Article Development

### Unique Portland Characteristics

1. **Scale**: With approximately 200 public stairways, Portland has one of America's most extensive urban stairway networks.

2. **Concentration**: The Alameda Ridge alone features 11 staircases with 844 total steps, creating one of the densest concentrations of public stairs in any American city.

3. **Historical Span**: Portland's stairways represent multiple construction eras—from the streetcar boom (1910-1920), through the WPA Depression era (1930s), to modern maintenance and reconstruction (1984 Washington Park rebuild).

4. **Worker Heritage**: Unlike purely recreational staircases, Portland's stairs served essential economic functions, connecting workers' homes to lumber mills, shipyards, and streetcar lines.

### Most Compelling Narratives

1. **The Linnton Story**: Workers popping out of their houses to reach the lumber mill in five minutes via staircase—then the bulldozing of downtown in the 1960s for Highway 30.

2. **The Hidden Tree**: An old stairway that fell into such disrepair that a seedling sprouted in a crack in the steps, now grown into a full-grown tree.

3. **The Alphabet District Pattern**: Every street between G and Q (except Hoyt) ending in a staircase—a systematic approach to topographical challenges.

4. **The 107th Avenue Paradox**: Possibly the only street in Portland that is actually a stairway.

5. **Urban Renewal's Impact**: Over 1,100 homes demolished in Albina by 1973, 450 homes destroyed for Veterans Memorial Coliseum, 3,000 people displaced from the Eliot neighborhood alone—yet many stairways survived.
