# The Manitou Springs Coffin Races

Every October, thousands of people pack the streets of Manitou Springs, Colorado, most of them in costume, to watch teams of mourners race homemade coffins down a 585-foot course. Five people per team: four pushers and one "Emma" riding inside the coffin. They compete for speed, sure. But also for Best Coffin, Best Emma, and Best Entourage.

The whole thing sounds like a fever dream cooked up by marketing consultants trying to capitalize on Halloween tourism. It isn't. The Emma Crawford Coffin Races honor a real woman whose coffin actually did race down a mountainside. Without anybody pushing it.

## The Woman on the Mountain

Emma Crawford moved to Manitou Springs in 1889, a twenty-four-year-old concert pianist from the East Coast. She came with her mother and sister, hopeful that the clean mountain air and healing mineral springs would cure her tuberculosis.

It didn't.

She died on December 4, 1891, at age twenty-six.

Before she died, Emma had accomplished a hike up nearby Red Mountain. Standing at the top, looking out over the valley, she made her wish known: when she passed, she wanted to be buried on that mountain.

Her fiancé and friends honored the request. They carried her body to the summit and buried her there.

## The First Burial

Red Mountain isn't particularly tall or imposing. But it's steep enough, rocky enough, and remote enough that burying someone at the top required serious effort. Emma's fiancé and twelve friends climbed the mountain carrying her coffin, dug a grave, and laid her to rest.

That should have been the end of the story.

## The Development Problem

The Louisville and Nashville Railroad Company had plans for Red Mountain. Development meant Emma's grave was in the way. So they moved her.

The second burial didn't receive the same care as the first. Historical records indicate she wasn't buried very deep—not covered with much more than loose dirt and rocks. No one thought much about it at the time.

Red Mountain gets harsh winters. Spring rains wash down the slopes. Year after year, the elements worked on that shallow grave.

## August 4, 1929

Two boys were hiking on Red Mountain when they found a human skull.

They also found a casket nameplate. It read: Emma L. Crawford.

In 1929, after years of harsh winters and spring rains, Emma and her coffin had come racing down the mountainside. The boys found only the casket handles, the nameplate, and a few bones. The coffin itself had mostly disintegrated during its unscheduled descent.

Emma Crawford's remains had spent thirty-eight years on Red Mountain before making their final, unintended journey.

## The Memorial

Emma was reburied—properly this time—in the Manitou Springs cemetery. Her grave is still there. The story of her coffin sliding down the mountain became local legend.

## The Race Begins

In the mid-1990s, members of the Manitou Springs Chamber of Commerce board sat around a kitchen table brainstorming ideas. They wanted something unique, something that would bring people to Manitou Springs, something that honored the town's quirky history.

Someone brought up Emma Crawford.

In 1995, the Manitou Springs Chamber of Commerce started the first coffin races in the nation, in memory of Emma. The event was part joke, part tribute, part tourism draw. It worked.

## The Rules

The race has specifications. They're strict about safety.

Each team consists of four runners to propel homemade coffins and one rider inside. The coffin must meet construction standards—it has to be safe enough to carry a person at speed down Manitou Avenue.

Pushers must hold onto the coffin until it crosses the finish line. Teams must stay in their own lanes. Two coffin teams compete in one heat. Fastest time wins that heat. Winners advance.

Race officials can disqualify participants whose coffins pose potential danger. This is important when you're sending people careening down a street in wooden boxes decorated to look like everything from pirate ships to spacecraft.

## The Costumes

The race is as much theater as competition. Teams compete in speed, coffin design, team names, and costumes. Awards go to fastest time, but also Best Coffin, Best Emma, and Best Entourage.

Over 10,000 spectators show up each year. Most of them wear costumes. The event happens annually on the weekend closest to Halloween, so the whole town transforms into a massive Halloween party built around racing coffins.

Before the races, there's a parade of coffins where teams showcase their creations and costumes. Pirates carry coffins shaped like ships. Aliens wheel metallic caskets. Zombies push gore-splattered boxes. Each Emma rides in their coffin wearing whatever costume matches their team's theme.

## The 585-Foot Course

The race happens on Manitou Avenue in downtown Manitou Springs. It's 585 feet—195 yards—of asphalt lined with thousands of spectators. Not particularly long. But when you're pushing a coffin with a person inside, racing against another team, trying not to veer into the crowd or flip the thing over, 585 feet is enough.

The fastest teams complete the course in under a minute. The slowest teams... well, the slowest teams are usually having more fun anyway.

## What It Means

Emma Crawford didn't ask for any of this. She asked to be buried on a mountain with a view. She got that, briefly, before railroad development and poor burial practices sent her coffin tumbling down the slope.

The race started as a Chamber of Commerce brainstorm. It could have been tacky. It could have been a cynical tourism grab disguised as memorial.

Instead, it became something else: a town-wide celebration of the absurd, a Halloween tradition that honors a real woman's story while fully embracing the ridiculous premise of racing coffins down a street. It's reverent and irreverent at once, which seems appropriate for someone whose final resting place refused to stay put for four decades.

Emma Crawford wanted to rest on a mountain. She got that, and then she got something else: an annual race where thousands of people dress up in costumes, build elaborate coffins, and run like hell down Manitou Avenue, all in her name.

There are worse ways to be remembered.
