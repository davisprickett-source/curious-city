# The Bakken Boom's Ghost Towns

For a brief, fevered stretch of years, western North Dakota looked like what prosperity was supposed to look like. Oil rigs studded the horizon. Men made six figures driving trucks. Hastily constructed housing developments sprouted from prairie soil like crops. The future had arrived, carried on the back of hydraulic fracturing, and it promised to last.

It didn't.

Today, those same housing developments stand empty. Thousand-bed man camps sit vacant, their modular units stacked like abandoned shipping containers. Towns that tripled in population within a decade are learning what it means to shrink just as fast. The Bakken oil boom created boom towns. Then it created ghosts.

## The Technology That Changed Everything

The Bakken Formation—a vast shale oil deposit underlying parts of North Dakota, Montana, and Saskatchewan—had been known since the 1950s. But the oil was trapped in rock, inaccessible with conventional drilling. For decades, it sat there, a fortune no one could touch.

Then came hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling.

By 2008, these technologies had advanced enough to make Bakken oil economically viable. Drilling in North Dakota surged. Oil production increased from slightly less than 100,000 barrels per day in 2007 to about 1,000,000 barrels per day in 2014. The boom peaked in 2012. For a few years, North Dakota rivaled Texas in oil production.

The price of oil stayed around $100 per barrel for most of the early 2010s. At those prices, fracking the Bakken made sense. Companies poured in. Workers followed.

## The Town That Doubled

Williston had 12,000 people in the late 2000s. Then the oil rigs arrived.

During the boom, the town's population nearly doubled. The 2010 census recorded 14,700 residents. By 2020, that number had grown to 27,700, making Williston the sixth-largest city in North Dakota. At the peak in 2015, the population was estimated at close to 40,000.

The Williston micropolitan statistical area, including the surrounding Williams County, grew from 22,398 people in 2010 to 40,950 in 2020—an 83% increase.

All those people needed places to live. Housing prices skyrocketed. Eviction filings in Williams County rose from 0.002 percent in 2010 to over 7 percent by 2019, as fracked oil production in the area grew from 300,000 barrels a month to 7.5 million barrels a month. Rents that once cost a few hundred dollars climbed to thousands. A parking spot with electrical hookup for an RV could run $1,000 a month.

The oil companies built man camps to house workers.

## The Man Camps

They had names: Target Lodge, Bear Paw Lodge, Williston North Lodge, Pioneer Lodge & Commons. They were modular housing developments, built quickly and cheaply, designed to hold hundreds or thousands of workers in dormitory-style accommodations.

Target Logistics—later renamed Target Hospitality—operated 3,700 beds across the Bakken, with its largest facilities in Tioga and Williston and expansions underway in Stanley. Pioneer Lodge & Commons near Watford City held 3,100 beds. Williston North Lodge housed 249 workers.

These weren't apartments. They were industrial housing, meant to pack in as many bodies as possible. Workers shared rooms, bathrooms, common areas. The camps provided food and transportation. They were self-contained ecosystems built around oil extraction.

During the boom, the man camps were full. Workers flew in from across the country, lured by the promise of high-paying jobs. Truck drivers made six figures. Rig workers pulled down $80,000 or more. Men with high school diplomas and no specialized training found themselves earning more than college graduates in coastal cities.

The work was brutal. Winter temperatures dropped below zero. The hours were long. But the money was real.

Until it wasn't.

## Watford City's Explosion

Watford City had 1,744 people at the 2010 census.

Within a year, the population grew to nearly 7,500. At the height of the boom, the city was likely serving 10,000 to 15,000 people living in the area. The peak population hit 7,817 in 2019.

Then came 2020. From 2019 to 2020, Watford City's population declined by 1,567 people—a 20.05% year-on-year decrease. The 2020 census recorded 6,207 residents.

The town had tripled in population within a decade. Then it began to shrink just as fast.

## The Bust

In 2014, oil prices crashed.

Crude that had been selling for $100 a barrel dropped to $50, then $30. The economics of fracking the Bakken, which required high prices to remain profitable, collapsed. Companies pulled out. Drilling stopped. Workers left.

Over 180 rigs had been drilling at the Bakken oil field in 2012. By 2015, only 27 remained active. More than 4,000 workers lost their jobs in the first quarter following the bust.

The man camps emptied. Target Logistics and other operators cut back on free housing, food, and air travel they'd once used to lure workers. Thousands of hastily constructed housing developments stood vacant. Towns that had been overwhelmed by growth suddenly faced empty buildings and declining tax revenue.

North Dakota had experienced five consecutive years as the number one state in personal-income growth and job creation. The building explosion that accompanied the boom hadn't lasted long enough to support all the construction. Municipalities and investors were left holding thousands of approved building units for people who no longer lived there.

The man camps became ghost camps.

## What Remains

Drive through western North Dakota today and you'll see the remnants. Modular housing units stacked in fields. Empty man camps with no one to fill them. Buildings constructed in haste, now waiting for a second boom that may never come.

Some towns adapted. Williston and Watford City invested boom-era revenue into infrastructure improvements. They built schools, expanded water and sewage systems, paved roads. When the workers left, at least the improvements remained.

But the ghost camps tell a different story. They're monuments to optimism that outpaced reality, to the belief that prosperity built on a single commodity would last.

## The Current State

As of early 2026, the Bakken is in a mature phase. Crude oil production has climbed back over 1.3 million barrels per day, still short of the pre-pandemic high of 1.5 million. The EIA forecasts that oil production from the Bakken will rise slightly at the end of 2025 before falling in 2026.

Continental Resources, the second-largest Bakken producer, accounting for 16 to 17 percent of total basin production, reduced its drilling rigs to nearly zero as of mid-January 2026. Most operators estimate output in coming years will remain nearly flat, with modest 1-2% increases at best.

The focus has shifted from growth to efficiency. The easy oil has been extracted. The infrastructure constraints and limited high-quality drilling sites mean the boom won't return in its previous form.

Some man camps have been dismantled. Others sit waiting, ready to be activated if prices rise again. The towns that grew too fast are learning to live smaller. Watford City's population continues to decline. Williston has stabilized but hasn't returned to boom-era numbers.

## The Lesson

The Bakken boom promised to last. Industry projections estimated years of growth, with oil prices staying high. North Dakota officials encouraged development, approved construction, welcomed the influx.

But commodity booms follow predictable patterns. They rise fast. They collapse faster.

Western North Dakota is dotted with the evidence: empty housing developments, man camps turned to ghost camps, towns learning to exist at half their peak population. The boom brought money, jobs, and hope. It also brought instability, inequality, and the kind of growth that's impossible to sustain.

The workers who came for six-figure salaries have mostly left. The companies that built the infrastructure moved on to the next boom. The people who lived there before the boom and stayed through the bust are the ones left to figure out what comes next.

The oil is still there, trapped in shale. The rigs could return if prices rise. But the lesson is written in those empty man camps, in the population charts showing Watford City's decline, in the ghost towns that bloom and die on commodity cycles.

Prosperity built on extraction is always temporary. The boom ends. The ghosts remain.
