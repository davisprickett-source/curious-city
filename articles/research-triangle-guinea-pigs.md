# Research Triangle's Guinea Pigs

The Raleigh-Durham area has world-class universities, cutting-edge research facilities, and a thriving biotechnology sector. It's also home to an informal economy of people who make a living letting pharmaceutical companies test drugs on their bodies.

They're called guinea pigs. Some prefer "professional research subjects." The industry prefers "healthy volunteers." Whatever the term, the reality is the same: people in the Research Triangle earn money—sometimes enough to live on—by participating in Phase I clinical trials that determine whether experimental drugs are safe enough to test on sick people.

The work pays. It carries risk. And it exists in the gap between what pharmaceutical companies need and what most people are willing to do.

## The Number: $3,000

That's what contract research organizations can pay for a participant's body for a three-week trial. Shorter studies pay less. A blood test for colon cancer screening might net $325 to $475 in electronic payment card compensation. A generic drug study could pay $500. The compensation varies depending on study type, duration, and requirements.

The math is simple: pharmaceutical companies need healthy people to test drugs before they test them on sick people. Healthy volunteers get paid for the risk.

## How It Works

Phase I trials determine the metabolic and pharmacologic actions of drugs in humans, as well as the side effects associated with various doses. These trials aren't about whether the drug works. They're about whether it's safe enough to find out if it works.

The pharmaceutical industry didn't always recruit healthy volunteers. Until the mid-1970s, Phase I trials were conducted on prisoners. When the use of prisoners in drug safety trials was banned in the United States, the North American pharmaceutical industry had to find a new population of healthy subjects to test an increasing number of compounds.

They created what became an informal economy. Some subjects volunteered regularly. Some became dependent on trial income for survival.

## The Triangle Advantage

The clinical research industry contributes over $1 billion to the economy of North Carolina. The Research Triangle—Raleigh, Durham, Chapel Hill—is a hub. There are 1,538 paid clinical trials available in Raleigh alone. Studies cover vaccine trials, cancer treatments, and other medical conditions. Both healthy volunteers and patients with specific conditions can participate.

Companies like PPD Inc. in Morrisville and Applied Analytical Industries in Durham have polished the industry's image. Their facilities resemble hotel rooms, designed to attract healthy volunteers for clinical trials. Comfortable accommodations make the pitch easier: spend a few weeks in a monitored environment, take experimental medication, get paid.

## The People

One twenty-one-year-old interviewed in Raleigh had been guinea-pigging since 1994. He started with a generic drug study that paid $500.

A forty-eight-year-old mother of three in the Triangle works various jobs and plots clinical studies into her calendar each year. She typically qualifies for four or five studies annually, including trials at PPD and a long-term AIDS study at a university clinic.

Paul Clough has participated in more than eighty drug tests in the past eleven years. Another participant logged at least fifty trials in six years.

These aren't one-time participants trying to make extra cash. They're professionals. They know which facilities pay better, which studies have the most manageable side effects, how to schedule trials to maximize income while minimizing risk.

## Who They Are

In Phase I research, the vast majority of participants are under-represented minorities. In the Northeast, they're typically Black men. In the Southwest, they're typically Hispanic men. The broader study of professional guinea pigs focuses on a group of poor, mostly white male Americans who earn a livelihood testing drug safety in clinical trials.

The demographic breakdown tells a story about who has access to traditional employment and who doesn't, about who can afford to decline $3,000 for three weeks of testing an experimental drug and who can't.

## The Risks

Clinics rely on healthy volunteers who get paid for taking the risk. That risk is real.

Phase I trials determine safety. That means no one knows what the side effects will be. Participants might experience nothing. They might experience nausea, headaches, dizziness, or worse. They're the first humans to find out what happens when the drug enters a body.

The facilities monitor participants closely. Medical staff observe for adverse reactions. If something goes wrong, treatment is available. But the entire point of Phase I trials is that they're testing unknown effects.

The frequency of participation adds another layer of risk. Someone who's participated in eighty trials over eleven years has exposed their body to eighty different experimental compounds. The long-term effects of that exposure aren't well studied because professional guinea pigs are, by definition, an informal population operating at the edges of medical research.

## The Industry

Contract research organizations like PPD built an industry around this need. PPD was founded by Fred Eshelman in 1985 as a one-person consulting firm. In 1986, he expanded the company's scope to include development services and relocated operations from Maryland to North Carolina. The company eventually relocated to Wilmington, North Carolina, but maintains a major facility in Morrisville in the Triangle.

PPD was one of the contract research pioneers founded in North Carolina in the early 1980s, alongside Quintiles (now IQVIA). The company was later acquired by Thermo Fisher Scientific and now operates as part of that organization, providing drug development, laboratory, and lifecycle management services to pharmaceutical, biotechnology, medical device, academic, and government organizations.

These companies transformed clinical research from an ad-hoc process into a streamlined industry. They built facilities designed to attract participants. They standardized compensation. They made professional guinea-pigging possible.

## The Database Economy

Multiple websites—PolicyLab, AllClinicalTrials.com, CenterWatch, ClinicalConnection—provide searchable databases of available paid clinical trials in the Raleigh-Durham area. You can filter by condition, compensation, duration, and location. The infrastructure exists to match experimental drugs with willing bodies efficiently.

Major institutions conducting clinical trials in the area include Duke Health, UNC Health Rex, and North Carolina Clinical Research. Between universities, hospitals, and private research firms, the supply of trials is steady. So is the demand for participants.

## The Lives

What does it look like to make a living as a professional guinea pig?

You track the databases. You know which facilities are recruiting for which studies. You know your medical history well enough to identify which trials you'll qualify for. You plan your calendar around inpatient stays—three weeks here, a weekend there, depending on the study requirements.

You show up. You get examined. Blood draws, vitals, medical history. If you qualify, you receive the experimental drug—pill, injection, infusion, whatever the study protocol requires. Then you wait. Medical staff monitor you. You can't leave. You read, watch TV, sleep, talk to other participants who are doing the same thing.

Side effects might happen. Usually they're manageable. Sometimes they're not. You report everything. That's the job.

When the study ends, you get paid. Then you look for the next one.

## The Ethics

The pharmaceutical industry needs Phase I trials. Drugs can't be tested on patients until someone determines basic safety. That requires testing on healthy people.

But the system creates a population of people who participate repeatedly, who depend on trial income, who expose themselves to experimental compounds dozens of times because the alternative is worse. It's a market transaction: companies need bodies, people need money, everyone consents.

Except consent becomes complicated when one party has significantly more power than the other, when participation is driven by economic necessity rather than pure volunteerism, when the long-term effects of repeated exposure to experimental drugs aren't studied because the participants exist in an informal economy that doesn't generate long-term data.

The facilities have gotten nicer. PPD's Morrisville facility looks like a hotel. The industry has polished its image. But underneath, the transaction remains: your body, their drug, compensation that makes the risk worth taking if you need the money badly enough.

## The Triangle

The Research Triangle has universities, research hospitals, pharmaceutical companies, and biotech firms. It has the infrastructure to develop drugs from concept to market. And it has the people willing to serve as the first human test subjects for those drugs.

Some of them are professionals. Some participate occasionally. All of them exist at the intersection of medical progress and economic necessity, where the need to know if a drug is safe meets the need to pay rent.

The clinical research industry contributes over a billion dollars to North Carolina's economy. Part of that value comes from people who've figured out how to make a living offering their bodies to pharmaceutical companies that need to know what happens when you put an experimental compound into a human being.

They're called guinea pigs. They prefer "professional research subjects." Either way, they're the ones who find out first whether the drug works or whether it doesn't, whether the side effects are manageable or whether they're not, whether the pharmaceutical company can proceed to Phase II or whether they need to go back to the lab.

Somebody has to go first. In the Research Triangle, there's an entire economy built around the people who do.
