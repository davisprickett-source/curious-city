# This Is the Place (But Whose Place?)

At the mouth of Emigration Canyon on the east side of Salt Lake City stands a sixty-foot monument. Mahonri M. Young—grandson of Brigham Young—sculpted it between 1939 and 1947. It was dedicated on July 24, 1947, exactly one hundred years after the Mormon pioneers entered the Salt Lake Valley.

The monument's name comes from what Brigham Young supposedly said upon first seeing the valley: "This is the right place, drive on."

The monument tells a story. Pioneer perseverance. Religious freedom. The founding of a new home after persecution and exodus. It's an origin story, cast in bronze and stone.

But it tells only one side.

## The Valley Before July 1847

The Salt Lake Valley wasn't empty when the Mormons arrived.

It also wasn't densely populated. The valley served as a buffer zone between the Shoshone to the north and the Ute to the south. The Goshutes lived to the west. Native groups traversed the valley occasionally but didn't establish permanent encampments there. They preferred more fertile valleys with better access to water and game.

Utah Lake, south of the Salt Lake Valley, was different. That area was permanently inhabited by the Timpanogos Utes—also called Tumpanawach or Lagunas by Europeans, who called them "fish-eaters." Utah Lake was one of the most permanent locations of any Ute community, a major trade crossroads and subsistence area.

When the first group of Mormon immigrants arrived in the Salt Lake Valley on July 22, 1847, after 111 days on the trail, they found a place inhabited but not densely settled. More than 20,000 Native people lived across what would become Utah Territory. Five tribes: Ute, Goshute, Shoshone, Paiute, and Navajo, whose ancestral homelands had been there for thousands of years.

The Mormons settled in what was essentially neutral ground between these groups.

At first, that worked.

## The Pattern

What happened in Utah Territory is the same story that occurred throughout the early history of the United States. Settlers arrived. Native people were displaced. In Utah, it happened quickly.

The initial settlement of the Salt Lake Valley didn't bother the Utes too much because the valley was considered a buffer zone. But Mormon settlement didn't stop at Salt Lake. Within two days of entering the valley, the pioneers erected a permanent settlement at City Creek, near modern-day Temple Square. Then they expanded.

Conflict didn't really begin until Mormons extended their settlements south into Utah Valley—into Timpanogos territory, into the area around Utah Lake. The best fishing site. The major trade crossroads. The subsistence area that had supported the Ute people for generations.

The pattern repeated: settlers arrived, and Native people were pushed from their fertile lands. The Saints offered some food to help compensate. It wasn't enough. Continual encroachment caused conflict that led to violence between the two groups.

## The Wars

Settlers and Native people clashed repeatedly at the mouth of the Provo River. Brigham Young countered Ute raiding with force. The Walker War lasted from 1853 to 1854. The Black Hawk War stretched from 1863 to 1868, though some sources date it from 1865 to 1872. Both wars revolved around Native subsistence raiding to avoid starvation.

The Black Hawk War involved an estimated 150 battles, skirmishes, raids, and military engagements, primarily between Mormon settlers in Sanpete County, Sevier County, and other parts of central and southern Utah, and members of sixteen Ute, Southern Paiute, Apache, and Navajo tribes. They were led by a local Ute war chief named Antonga Black Hawk.

Contributing factors: a local drought in 1864, food shortages in Mormon settlements, and the U.S. Indian agent's failure to provide enough supplies to Utes on the new Uintah Reservation brought many native bands to the brink of starvation. In winter 1864-1865, a smallpox epidemic swept through Ute bands. Chief Arapeen was among the casualties.

## February 23, 1865

President Abraham Lincoln signed "An Act to extinguish the Indian Title to Lands in the Territory of Utah suitable for agricultural and mineral Purposes." The act expropriated Native lands in Utah Territory outside of the Uinta Valley Reservation.

On June 6, 1865, a council of the Ute people was called at Spanish Fork Reservation. The aged leader Sowiette explained that the Ute people did not want to sell their land and go away. He asked why the groups couldn't live on the land together. Sanpitch also spoke against the treaty.

Advised by Brigham Young that these were the best terms they could get, the leaders signed.

The treaty provided that the Utes give up their lands in central Utah, including the Corn Creek, Spanish Fork, and San Pete Reservations. Only the Uintah Valley Reservation remained. In 1865, the Mormons forced the starving remnants of the Timpanogos to sign a treaty and move to a distant reservation.

An Act of Congress in 1864 had already required Utes to give up all land and title rights and move to Uintah Reservation within a year.

## The Numbers

Between 1847 and 1900, the Native population of Utah suffered an estimated 90% decrease.

This wasn't natural attrition. It was dispossession, starvation, disease, and violence. The Book of Mormon promised that Native people would be redeemed through the influence of the gospel. The reality of the frontier situation in Utah demanded their immediate displacement instead.

The pattern was efficient. Settlers extended their settlements north and south along the front range. Native people were displaced from traditional subsistence areas. Conflict increased. The displaced raided to avoid starvation. The settlers responded with force. Treaties were signed under duress. Reservations were established in less fertile, more remote areas. The Native population collapsed.

## The Monument

The This Is the Place Monument doesn't mention any of this.

It honors the Mormon pioneers who entered the Salt Lake Valley on July 24, 1847. It celebrates the founding of what would become Salt Lake City and, eventually, the state of Utah. It commemorates religious freedom, perseverance, and the building of a community in what the monument's narrative describes as an empty or unused land.

The monument is beautiful. Mahonri Young's sculpture is impressive. The dedication on the hundredth anniversary of the pioneers' arrival was meaningful to the community that traces its heritage to those 1847 settlers.

But the monument tells an incomplete story.

## The Heritage Park

In the mid-1970s, a living history museum called This Is the Place Heritage Park was built adjacent to the monument. The 450-acre site offers visitors a look at life in nineteenth-century Utah from the Mormon arrival in 1847 to statehood in 1896.

The park includes a Native American Village that honors the contributions of the five tribes—Shoshone, Paiute, Ute, Goshute, and Navajo—who inhabited the area long before the pioneers arrived. This addition acknowledges what the monument itself does not: the valley wasn't empty, and the story of Utah's founding involved more than one group of people.

## Whose Place?

"This is the right place" is a claim about belonging. About home. About a promised land found after exile and persecution.

For the Mormon pioneers in 1847, the Salt Lake Valley was that place. They built a city. They irrigated the desert. They created a community that endured.

But a home gained for one group meant a home lost for another.

The Ute, Shoshone, Paiute, Goshute, and Navajo peoples had been there for thousands of years. Utah Lake was the Timpanogos Utes' permanent home. The Salt Lake Valley was neutral ground, yes, but it was part of a larger landscape that sustained interconnected communities for generations.

The story the monument tells is true: the Mormon pioneers did arrive in 1847, they did build a city, they did face hardships and persevere. But that's not the only truth.

The other truth is that between 1847 and 1900, the Native population of Utah decreased by 90%. The other truth is that by 1865, the Utes had been forced onto a single reservation, their lands expropriated by an act signed by Abraham Lincoln. The other truth is that the wars fought between settlers and Native peoples were caused by starvation, displacement, and the systematic removal of people from lands they had inhabited for millennia.

## The Question

Whose place is it?

The monument says it's the place Brigham Young identified for his people. That's accurate.

It's also the place where the Ute, Shoshone, Paiute, Goshute, and Navajo peoples lived before Mormon settlement. The place where the Timpanogos Utes fished at Utah Lake. The place where, within fifty-three years of Mormon arrival, the Native population collapsed by 90%.

The monument tells one side of the story. It's an important side, meaningful to the descendants of those 1847 pioneers. But standing at the mouth of Emigration Canyon, looking at sixty feet of bronze and stone commemorating an arrival, it's worth remembering what that arrival meant for the people who were already there.

"This is the right place, drive on."

For some, it was. For others, it was the beginning of displacement, starvation, and loss on a scale that nearly erased them from the landscape entirely.

The monument should probably mention that.
