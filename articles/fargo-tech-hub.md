# Why Is Fargo a Tech Hub Now?

The question itself sounds absurd. Fargo. North Dakota. A place most Americans know only from a Coen Brothers film where people talk funny and a wood chipper eats evidence. Tech hub?

Yet here's the thing no one tells you about American innovation: it doesn't always bloom where you expect it. Sometimes it grows in places where the cost of a mortgage won't devour your salary, where traffic means waiting through two light cycles instead of two hours, where the people building the future can actually afford to live in it.

Fargo didn't become a tech hub by accident. It became one because someone bet a quarter-million dollars of farmland on it.

## The Bet That Changed Everything

In 1983, Doug Burgum mortgaged $250,000 worth of North Dakota farmland to buy into a small accounting software company called Great Plains Software. He acquired a 2.5% stake and became vice president of marketing. The company had been founded two years earlier by Joseph Larson, and Burgum found it because his mother and Larson's mother worked together.

The next year, Burgum led a group of investors—mostly relatives—who purchased controlling interest in Great Plains. The transaction took three weeks from start to close. The Burgums put $2 million into the company, making it their second family business.

Over the next seventeen years, Burgum grew Great Plains from 250 employees to a company generating $300 million in annual sales. During the 1980s, Fortune magazine often ranked it among the nation's top 100 companies to work for.

In May 2001, Microsoft acquired Great Plains Software for $1.1 billion. At the time, it was the most expensive acquisition Microsoft had ever made.

That acquisition planted something in Fargo's soil that still bears fruit today.

## The Campus That Stayed

The Great Plains headquarters became Microsoft Fargo. Instead of consolidating operations and moving everyone to Redmond, Microsoft kept the Fargo campus running. The first building, called Horizon, had broken ground in 1997 and opened in September 1999. By the time Microsoft acquired the company, the campus was already taking shape.

Microsoft didn't just maintain the Fargo presence. It expanded it. In 2007, the company announced a 185,000-square-foot expansion, adding capacity for 575 more employees. Additional buildings followed: Vision, completed around 2009, and the Commons building around the same time. The campus now spans four buildings.

Today, Microsoft Fargo employs over 1,600 people. They work on research, customer service, accounting, sales, and business solutions like Microsoft Dynamics. The campus has become a powerhouse in the company's business applications division.

But Microsoft's presence did something more important than create jobs. It created proof of concept.

## The Ecosystem Takes Root

In 2013, Emerging Prairie began telling the stories of entrepreneurs and startups in the Fargo region. The founders had seen other areas of the country highlighting their entrepreneurial communities and believed Fargo needed to do the same. They started as a digital media company, sharing news about founders and innovation.

Then people needed space to meet and work. In 2015, Emerging Prairie opened a co-working space—an unanticipated addition to the organization—as the startup community continued to grow. It became a place where startup founders, independent workers, and engaged community members could gather.

In 2017, a group of entrepreneurs brainstormed ways to bring together startups, investors, and the people who support them. Greg Tehven, co-founder and CEO of Emerging Prairie, helped drive the creation of Prairie Capital Summit.

In 2018, at a 1 Million Cups Fargo event, Barry Batcheller, founder of Appareo Systems, asked a question: "What is Fargo's major?"

The answer came a year later. In 2019, Emerging Prairie launched Grand Farm, an agriculture technology initiative. They partnered with Microsoft and dozens of other organizations, deciding that agtech was the future of the region.

The timing made sense. North Dakota sits in one of the most productive agricultural regions in the world. The technology to track, optimize, and revolutionize farming was emerging. Fargo had the tech talent and the agricultural knowledge. Grand Farm became a world-class ag innovation center, pairing Microsoft technology with North Dakota farmers and entrepreneurs.

## The Success Stories

Microsoft wasn't the only tech company to make it work in Fargo.

Appareo Systems has become a leader in electronic and computer products for aerospace and defense. They developed the AIRS-400 flight recorder, which captures 4K Ultra HD video and weighs just 11 ounces. They've crushed it in the unmanned aerial vehicle space. In 2024, the FAA awarded them an $802,599 grant for groundbreaking research in radio functionality and command control systems. AGCO, one of the biggest names in agricultural implements, recently acquired them.

Bushel, cofounded by Jake Joraanstad and Ryan Raguse in 2017, built software for what they call "the messy middle" of agriculture—the agribusinesses, co-ops, retailers, and large grain companies that connect farmers to markets. The company grew from a team of two or three people to 110 employees, with over half working on the product. Today, over 2,600 grain and ag retail facilities and 110,000 farmers across the U.S. and Canada use Bushel's tools daily. The platform facilitates contracts, grain sales, and payments between farmers, grain buyers, ag retailers, protein producers, and food companies.

North Dakota's tech industry added $2.1 billion to the state economy in 2021, employing over 17,000 people.

## The Microsoft TechSpark Initiative

Three years ago, Microsoft President Brad Smith joined North Dakota Governor Doug Burgum—yes, the same Doug Burgum who founded Great Plains Software—in downtown Fargo to launch Microsoft TechSpark. The civic initiative was formed to foster economic impact and job creation through partnerships and collaboration.

TechSpark represents Microsoft's commitment to rural technology development. Grand Farm leads a revolution in agtech through this partnership, creating innovations that serve farmers locally and globally.

## The Math That Actually Works

Here's what nobody tells you about tech salaries: they're relative.

The average software engineer in Fargo makes $123,174 per year, which is 17% lower than the national average. Entry-level software engineers earn around $79,930 per year.

In Silicon Valley, the average starting salary for a software engineer is $111,000, with Google employees receiving an average annual market salary of $202,000, including signing bonuses, annual bonuses, and annual equity.

Sounds like Silicon Valley wins, right?

Not when you factor in cost of living.

Fargo's cost of living is 6% lower than the national average. Housing expenses are 17% lower than the national average. Utility prices are 9% lower.

The San Francisco Bay Area is one of the most expensive places to live in the United States, with an average home price over $700,000. Groceries cost about 30% higher than the U.S. average, with a family of four spending around $1,288 per month on food alone. A single, entry-level software engineer in Silicon Valley can expect to spend more than half their after-tax income on housing costs alone.

In Fargo, a software engineer making $120,000 can buy a house, save money, and build wealth. In Silicon Valley, a software engineer making $200,000 can rent a two-bedroom apartment and hope the landlord doesn't raise the rent next year.

The math is simple. The purchasing power is real.

## The Quiet Advantage

Fargo's tech hub didn't emerge from a government incentive program or a billionaire's pet project. It emerged from one man's willingness to bet farmland on software, Microsoft's decision to honor that legacy, and a community's choice to build on what they'd started.

The tight-knit community fosters collaboration and mentorship through organizations like Emerging Prairie. Tech professionals work on meaningful problems—feeding the world, building aerospace technology, creating software that moves agriculture into the future.

And they do it in a place where a mortgage doesn't require a six-figure downpayment, where traffic means a ten-minute commute, where winter is brutal but summers compensate, and where the cost of ambition doesn't include sacrificing your ability to afford the life you're building.

Silicon Valley taught the world that tech innovation requires expensive real estate and venture capital. Fargo is quietly proving that wrong. One farmer's bet, one Microsoft campus, one startup at a time.

The question isn't why Fargo became a tech hub.

The question is why more places haven't figured out the same thing.
